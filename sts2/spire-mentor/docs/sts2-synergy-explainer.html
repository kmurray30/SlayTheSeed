Is that synergy real?

How we measure card synergy causally in Slay the Spire 2 — and why co-occurrence stats (and even win-rate splits) will lie to you.

From a hobby project that rebuilt the STS2 game logic as a fast simulator with an AI agent that plays full runs. All charts below use illustrative numbers, not measured results — this post is about the method.

Every Spire player carries a mental synergy catalog: these two cards are a package, that one's a trap without its enabler. Community data — thousands of uploaded run histories — looks like the perfect way to test that folklore. And the first thing everyone computes is: which cards get drafted together?

The problem: co-draft statistics measure what players believe, not what works. If everyone drafts two cards together because a streamer said so, they'll co-occur beautifully whether or not they win games. To do better you need two upgrades: a definition of synergy that isn't correlation, and a way to run actual experiments. Having a full simulator of the game gives us the second part — we can force any deck into existence and make it fight.

1 · Synergy is an interaction, not a correlation

Here's the definition we use. Take a deck D, and some outcome measure V (win probability works). Then the synergy of cards A and B is the second difference:

S(A, B) = V(D + A + B) − V(D + A) − V(D + B) + V(D)

In words: add both cards, and subtract off what each card contributes alone. What's left is the part that only exists when they're together. The subtraction is load-bearing — without it, any pair of two individually strong cards looks "synergistic" when they're really just two good cards sharing a deck.

Crucially, we can measure each of those four cells experimentally: build the four decks in the simulator, run each through the same set of fights with the same random seeds, and count wins. No confounding from who chose to draft what — nobody chose anything; we forced the decks.

2 · Belief vs. reality: the four quadrants

Now combine two independent axes for every card pair: how strongly humans co-draft it (from community run data), and the causal interaction S (from simulator experiments). Every pair lands in one of four quadrants:

ConfirmedyesyesThe community is right — a real package.
MythyesnoTwo cards that are each fine (or famous) but add nothing to each other. Folklore.
UndiscoverednoyesA real engine the meta hasn't found (or unfairly dismissed).

The whole point of the pipeline is separating myth from confirmed — and occasionally digging an undiscovered gem out of the data.

3 · A trap for run-data analysts: survivorship in "wins vs. losses"

If you analyze public run histories, the obvious upgrade over co-draft counts is an outcome split: compute your favorite statistic separately on winning and losing runs and take the difference. We did exactly that in an early version. Don't.

The issue is truncation: losing runs end early. A deck that died on floor 15 never got its act 2 and act 3 picks. So its final decklist is small and missing every late-game card — not because those cards are bad, but because the run was already over.

So a naive wins-vs-losses statistic partly measures when in the run a pair gets assembled, not whether it wins. Rare pairs make it worse (a handful of losing decks decide the "loss side" of the estimate). Our fix on the observational side is a logistic regression with an explicit interaction term — won ~ A + B + A×B + ascension + deck size — which at least controls for the obvious confounds. But we treat even that as a candidate generator. Truth comes from experiments.

4 · Beyond pairs: archetypes

Real decks aren't built from pairs. "Shiv deck" or "strength stacking" are packages of 5–10 cards with enablers, payoffs, and support. Pairwise analysis structurally misses these: in a three-piece engine, sometimes no single pair is impressive but the trio is.

We discover candidate archetypes from data in two independent ways, per character:

  • From human runs: factorize the (deck × card) matrix of thousands of final decklists into a small number of components — each component is a weighted card group that tends to appear as a unit. This view knows what humans actually build, folklore included.
  • From the causal graph: take every pair that passed the interaction test, treat pairs as edges in a graph, and find densely connected communities. This view only contains verified interactions, but is blind to anything not yet tested.

Where both views agree, we have a human-played, causally-grounded archetype. A group only humans produce is folklore-until-validated; a group only the causal graph produces is a candidate undiscovered engine. Same quadrant idea, one level up.

5 · Is the archetype more than the sum of its parts? Dose-response.

For a 6-card package you can't test every subset — the combinatorics explode and the estimates drown in noise. Instead we borrow a tool from medicine: the dose-response curve. Inject 0, 1, 2, … k cards from the archetype into a base deck and measure win rate at each dose. If the archetype is a real engine, the curve should bend upward: the third piece is worth more than the first.

Two controls keep this honest:

  • Additive baseline: what the curve would look like if each card just contributed its solo value. Super-additivity = the measured curve pulling away from this line.
  • Matched control arm: inject the same number of cards of similar individual strength and rarity, but from no particular archetype. This kills the boring explanation "more good cards = more wins."

6 · The validation ladder: cheap guesses, expensive proof

Testing everything with full experiments would take forever, so candidates climb a ladder — each rung more expensive and more trustworthy than the last:

Rung 1 · screen

Neural value net

Our run-playing AI (trained with reinforcement learning) contains a value network: a learned "how likely is this run to win?" estimator. Querying it with modified decks gives instant, free dose-response curves.

milliseconds per deck · thousands of candidates · trust: low (the net has blind spots)

Rung 2 · confirm

Forced-deck combat sims

Actually build each dose's deck and fight it through a fixed panel of encounters, thousands of seeded fights per arm, with matched control arms. Real game logic, real variance.

minutes per candidate · hundreds of candidates · trust: high, at fight level

Rung 3 · prove

Whole-run injection A/B

Force-add the package (or a control package) into the deck at the start of hundreds of full runs and let the AI play the entire run normally. Nothing else differs — a genuine randomized experiment at run scale.

hours per candidate · a handful of finalists · trust: the real thing

One subtlety worth stealing for your own experiments: on rung 3 we inject cards rather than biasing the AI's draft policy toward the archetype. Biasing the policy changes a hundred downstream decisions and you end up measuring "is the policy change good," not "is the archetype good." Injection changes exactly one thing.

Why not just k-th order differences? The pairwise formula generalizes to triples and beyond, but a k-th difference is an alternating sum of 2k noisy win-rate estimates — its variance explodes and the sign becomes a coin flip. Dose-response asks a weaker but answerable question: does value accelerate as the package assembles? That's one curve against one baseline, and the noise stays manageable.

7 · What's next: upgrades from an idea tournament

To stress-test this design we ran a bracket-style review: idea generators working from different expert perspectives (causal inference, statistical genetics, recommender systems, experiment design, …) each proposed improvements, a judge panel seeded the field, and head-to-head matches picked winners. The podium is worth understanding in depth — every one of these is a transferable technique if you work with run data.

ChampionMultiple-testing control for the whole catalog

Here's a problem hiding in plain sight, in our pipeline and in most community stat sheets: rank thousands of card pairs by any statistic, and the top of the list is guaranteed to look amazing — even if no synergy exists at all. Test 5,000 pairs at the usual "95% confident" threshold and you expect ~250 false positives from pure luck. Our catalog tests thousands of pairs, for five characters, with several estimators each — and the original design confirmed a pair whenever a single confidence interval cleared zero. Geneticists hit this exact wall decades ago (testing a million gene variants against a disease) and the field's answer became standard practice: don't ask "is this pair significant?", ask "of everything I'm about to call real, what fraction is noise?" — the false discovery rate.

The mechanics are two-fold. First, a permutation null: recompute the entire synergy analysis after shuffling which cards are which (within groups of similar card strength, so the shuffle only destroys pairing information, not quality information). Whatever "synergy" the shuffled data shows is by construction pure luck — and it gives you the exact distribution of luck for your statistic on your data, no textbook assumptions needed. Compare the real scores against that null and you can say: beyond this cutoff we see 40 pairs but chance alone would produce 12, so roughly 30% of our "discoveries" out there are noise.

Second, shrinkage. A pair seen in 60 decks has a wildly noisy estimate, and noisy estimates that happen to swing high are exactly what ends up at the top of ranked lists (the "winner's curse" — the reason the best-looking pair in any leaderboard usually disappoints on re-measurement). Empirical-Bayes shrinkage pulls each pair's estimate toward the average behavior of the cards involved, in proportion to how noisy it is: well-measured pairs barely move, small-sample outliers get reined in. Pairs sharing a card borrow statistical strength from each other. The combination is nearly free to compute — it's post-processing on numbers we already have — and it changes what "confirmed" means from "cleared one test" to "survived correction for everything we tried."

The judges' reasoning for crowning it: it fixes a defect that silently corrupts every other part of the pipeline — the discovery views, the quadrant labels, and the allocation of expensive experiment budget, which would otherwise be spent chasing statistical ghosts.

Runner-upRead the AI's learned card geometry

Our run-playing AI was trained by reinforcement learning to win runs, and to do that it had to learn an internal representation of every card: each card is a vector of a few hundred numbers, tuned by nothing except "did this help win?" Cards the AI treats as interchangeable end up close together in that space; cards with different roles end up far apart. That geometry was expensive to learn — and it's sitting there, free to read.

Two uses. The first is a third archetype-discovery view: cluster the card vectors and you get the agent's implicit deck-building taxonomy, to set alongside the human view (what players draft together) and the causal view (what tests as super-additive). Where all three agree, confidence is high; where the agent's view disagrees with the human view, something interesting is going on — either the agent knows something players don't, or vice versa, and both are worth investigating.

The second use is quieter but won the judges over: picking control cards. Recall that dose-response experiments need control arms of "equally good but unrelated" cards — and choosing those by hand is the shakiest part of the whole design (match on rarity and cost and you can still accidentally pick a control that secretly belongs to the package, or one that's uselessly weak). Embedding geometry gives a principled matcher: choose controls that are near the archetype's cards in the space (similar role, similar power level) but outside the package cluster. Same neighborhood, different family.

One honest caveat: embedding closeness mostly encodes substitutability ("these do the same job"), not complementarity ("these amplify each other") — so it's strongest as a control-matcher and a provenance signal, with the causal ladder still deciding what's real. And rarely-drafted cards have undertrained, unreliable vectors, so frequency gating matters.

SemifinalistCard offers as a natural experiment

Everything observational we described so far shares one incurable disease: skilled players both draft better cards and win more for unrelated reasons, so "decks with X win more" never separates the card from the pilot. But run histories contain something better than what players picked: what they were offered. And offers are random draws from the reward pool.

That randomness is a gift. Whether card A ever showed up in your reward screens this run is a coin flip the player doesn't control — but it strongly influences whether A ends up in the deck. Meanwhile, the coin flip itself has no other path to affecting the run's outcome. In causal-inference terms, the offer is an instrumental variable — the same trick economists use when they can't randomize (a draft lottery, a policy cutoff), and epidemiologists call an encouragement design.

Concretely: compare runs where A was offered against runs where it never was — that difference is untouched by skill, because the RNG doesn't know who's playing. Scale it by how much being offered raises ownership, and you have the causal effect of having the card, from observational data alone. For synergy, instrument the pair: runs where both A and B happened to be offered vs. runs where only one was. There's even a built-in placebo test: cards that were offered but declined should show no effect — if they do, the randomness assumption is broken and you know not to trust the estimate.

The honest caveats: staple cards that everyone always takes leave too little offered-but-declined variation to work with (the "weak instrument" problem), and the method estimates the effect for players whose decision the offer actually changed — not for everyone. But this is the only rung of the ladder that gets causality out of human play, where skill-styles and metas live. If you analyze public run files, this one is stealable today.

SemifinalistLeave-one-out replay of real decks

Forced-deck experiments have a quiet weakness: they bolt cards onto tidy base decks that never occur in real play. A synergy measured in that lab setting might not survive contact with the messy 30-card decks people actually pilot — the classic distribution-shift trap (we've been burned by it in this project before, in a different experiment).

The complement: take real decks from real runs, replay their fights in the simulator with the exact same random seed, then re-run each fight with a single card deleted from the deck. Same fight, same shuffle, same enemy rolls — one card of difference. The swing in outcome is that card's marginal contribution in that exact context.

Card names above are placeholders from the classic game — the method is what matters.

Do this across hundreds of decks and thousands of fights, then split each card's Δ by whether a suspected partner was in the deck: if Inflame's marginal value is consistently larger when Heavy Blade is around, that's synergy measured where it actually lives — inside realistic decks, at realistic deck sizes, against the fights those decks actually faced.

The caveat is philosophical but real: deleting a card from a finished run is a slightly impossible counterfactual — the player who never took that card would have drafted differently afterward. So the number is a within-fight marginal, not a full alternate-timeline answer, and it complements rather than replaces the forced-deck experiments. The two designs fail in different ways, which is exactly why you want both.

Honorable mentionsFour more worth stealing

  • A held-out prediction gate. The catalog's ultimate exam: on runs it has never seen, do its confirmed synergies predict outcomes better than card quality alone? If not, the whole edifice isn't earning its keep. Cheap to run, brutal, and it turns "we believe our method" into a measured number.
  • The neural-net screen as a control variate (CUPED). The cheap value-net prediction and the expensive simulation answer are correlated — so subtract the prediction from the measurement and analyze the residual. Same unbiased answer, a fraction of the variance, meaning the same simulation budget resolves much smaller effects. Industry A/B platforms live on this trick; it transfers to game experiments unchanged.
  • An anti-synergy axis. Everything above hunts for positive interactions, but negative ones are just as actionable: two cards that compete for the same role or resource are worth a "skip — you already have this covered" hint. The same second-difference machinery finds them; we were simply discarding the negative tail.
  • Active-learning triage. With several cheap estimators (co-draft, regression, value net) and one expensive oracle (simulation), spend the oracle where the cheap estimators disagree the most — that's where a measurement changes beliefs. Agreement is cheap confidence; disagreement is information waiting to be bought.

8 · Takeaways if you analyze run data

  • Co-occurrence measures belief. It's genuinely useful — as a crowdsourced hypothesis list, not as evidence.
  • Beware truncation. Losing runs end early; any statistic split by win/loss inherits deck-size and run-length bias. Control for it, or better, condition on comparable run states.
  • Synergy needs a subtraction. If your metric doesn't subtract the parts, you're measuring "both cards are good."
  • Packages, not pairs. Dose-response with an additive baseline and matched controls scales to archetypes; higher-order interaction terms don't.
  • Simulators turn correlations into experiments. Once you can force decks into existence and replay identical seeds, most confounders simply vanish.
  • Thousands of tests need multiplicity control. Rank card pairs by any statistic and the top of the list is part signal, part luck. Permutation nulls and false-discovery-rate corrections tell you how much of each.
